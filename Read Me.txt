This study presents a Level Set Method-based model of sediment-driven island evolution and land reclamation for the Meghna Estuary, where the shoreline is considered as the zero level set of an implicit function, evolved in time according to a normal velocity field. The latter consists of a reference growth rate (derived from observations) and a tidal constituent, with a nonlinear river discharge term and a spatially varying weight function (accounting for spatially variable erosion-accretion potential) added. The obtained equation was found to be a bounded first-order nonlinear hyperbolic PDE, which does not exhibit spurious long-term drift due to the tidal constituent, as the mean value of the latter over a tidal cycle is zero, satisfying the Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy criterion.

Using this model, this study projected the areal extent and spatial distribution of four large islands in the estuary, namely, Urir Char, Sandwip, Swarna Dwip, and Bhasan Char, up to 2050. All four islands were found to contribute to the overall growth of the deltaic islands in the study period, with Swarna Dwip exhibiting the largest absolute growth (87.5 km$^2$), followed by Bhasan Char (30.5 km$^2$) and Urir Char (18.9 km$^2$), with Sandwip being the smallest (5.7 km$^2$). Overall, the total area of the four islands was projected to grow from approx.\ 813.14 km$^2$ in 2026 to approx.\ 955.67 km$^2$ in 2050. The growth was found to be primarily linear with minor nonlinear trends, which can be attributed to \rev{the periodic tidal modulation of the growth rate (Corollary~2), which displaces the cumulative area from the mean-rate line by a bounded amount}. Notably, a grid-based level-set simulation suggested that the progressive areal growth of the four islands could result in reductions in the widths of the waterways between them, potentially leading to their complete closure within several decades, facilitating the merging of the adjacent islands into a larger one or ones.
The mathematical methodology was validated in Sect.~4.6.1. It was proved that the proposed shoreline-normal velocity (with the perimeter-type normalisation $\ell_W$) reproduces the satellite-derived area-growth rate exactly (Proposition~1); that the model has a closed-form solution for a circular island, which the numerical scheme reproduced with a relative area error below 0.12\,\% on all tested grids; and that the tidal term can shift the merging time of two islands by at most $2a/\omega\approx2.9$ years (Proposition~3). The simulated areas of Table~4 are consistent with the closed-form model to within 3.3\,\% for each island.
Overall, the results of this study demonstrate the potential applicability of the Level Set Method in geomorphological modeling, particularly in the context of the dynamic and complex systems such as the Meghna Estuary deltaic islands, as it requires minimal assumptions about the system under study and allows for relatively straightforward implementation of complex geometries and topological changes such as island merging. At the same time, the current study has several key limitations that need to be addressed in future research, primarily the insufficient consideration of the processes involved due to the reliance on a few key observations as the basis for the model. In particular, the simplified consideration of the processes affecting the growth and evolution of the deltaic islands is a major limitation, as the proposed model does not consider wave effects, seasonal variations in river outflow, and storm surges, only accounting for the mean values of these processes. The weight function, the normal velocity, and the constants used in the model were similarly chosen primarily for simplicity rather than based on observations.
The validation presented here is limited to idealised analytical benchmarks; it verifies the mathematics and the numerical implementation, but not the physical parameters or the weight function, which require independent field data.
As such, the results of this study, particularly those presented in Sect.~4, should be considered as plausible scenarios rather than definite projections. Future work should focus on elaborating on this initial but somewhat simplistic model to develop a more physically accurate description of the system while addressing the uncertainty using sensitivity analysis.

Finally, there is a need for further validation of the model using additional field survey data, in particular, information regarding water levels, currents, turbidity, and bathymetry, in the Meghna Estuary, as well as considering alternative scenarios for the system evolution using the proposed level-set-based framework. In particular, future case studies could consider additional factors affecting the dynamics of the estuary and the islands, such as the effects of waves, storms, monsoon season, and seasonal variability. Adding a sediment transport module to the model could allow for further process-based considerations, providing additional insight into the evolution of the studied system. More broadly, additional studies are needed to consider the application of this approach in different estuaries and deltas to elaborate on the generalizability of the findings and consider potential local adjustments to the approach, if necessary. With these improvements, the Level Set Method, as demonstrated in this study, could become a powerful tool for investigating and projecting the potential evolution of estuarine deltaic islands, including those in the Meghna Estuary.
